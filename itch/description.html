April 1944. The Luftwaffe's operations staff weighs three targets for its new weapon, the Mistel: Gibraltar, the Soviet fleet at Leningrad, and the British Home Fleet in Scapa Flow. A note at the foot of the study settles it in one line: "bleibt Scapa Flow", remains Scapa Flow.

Dragon's Lair, 1944–45 is a hypothetical campaign study built on that file and on the war diary of February 1945, when the plan came back as Operation Drachenhöhle, Dragon's Lair, and was dropped within four days. It is a sequel to Iron Hammer, 1945. It does not ask whether the attack could have changed the war. It follows the staff's own arithmetic, eight Mistel against "no more than at most 2 battleships and 1–2 carriers", and sets it beside the Home Fleet's war diary, which records where every ship lay on every day.

Two ways through the same ten chapters.

  • Read the study: a dossier with the file in facsimile, full transcription and English translation, the war diary of February 1945, the British interrogation of the officer who was to direct Dragon's Lair, the fleet at anchor, and a critical reflection. At the end, the file's own plan is computed.
  • Take the staff's seat: decide on the airfield, how many combinations, how to come in, whether to photograph the anchorage first, when to strike, and in February 1945 whether to fly Dragon's Lair. A model runs your plan against the ships that were actually there.

A source finding. The estimate of two battleships and one or two carriers was wrong for the weeks in which the file was written, when Scapa Flow held up to seven battleships. It was right in late June 1944, when the weapon was due and the carriers had sailed for the Far East. In February 1945 the Flow held a single old battleship. And the date of the plan is not "late 1944", as some later accounts have it, but April 1944 and February 1945.

What the file does not count. Two chapters follow the Churchill Barriers, built to close the eastern entrances after a U-boat sank the Royal Oak inside the Flow in 1939 with the loss of 833 men, and the Italian prisoners of war who built them, struck against the work, and lived in camps at the edge of the anchorage the file meant to attack.

Every figure is linked. The file's numbers, the war diary's and the study's assumptions are marked as such. Plates: the range map from the file, German target dossiers of 1939–41, an Admiralty chart of 1944, Royal Navy and US Navy photographs, and recent photographs of the barriers and the Italian Chapel.
